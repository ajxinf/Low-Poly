# Sources: Broughton Suspension Bridge collapse, 12 April 1831

| Claim in script | Source |
|---|---|
| Bridge over the River Irwell, Salford/near Manchester, opened 1826, collapsed 12 April 1831 | [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broughton_Suspension_Bridge); [Grace's Guide](https://gracesguide.co.uk/Broughton_Suspension_Bridge) |
| Suspension bridges were the "new wonder of the age"; bridge a source of local pride | Wikipedia (citing Manchester Evening News) |
| 74 men of the 60th Rifle Corps, returning to barracks from exercises on Kersal Moor, four abreast | Wikipedia (citing Manchester Guardian, 16 April 1831) |
| Felt it vibrate in time with their steps, found it amusing, whistled a marching tune and stepped to "humour it" | Wikipedia; [Amusing Planet](https://www.amusingplanet.com/2023/04/marching-soldiers-and-collapsing-bridges.html) |
| Sound "resembling an irregular discharge of firearms"; iron column fell; corner dropped 16 or 18 ft | Wikipedia |
| About 40 thrown into water about 2 ft deep; no deaths; 20 injured | Wikipedia |
| A single badly forged bolt failed; vibration brought it on but it would have failed eventually | Wikipedia (investigation summary) |
| British Army ordered troops to break step on bridges; signs still exist (e.g. Albert Bridge, London) | Wikipedia; Amusing Planet (Albert Bridge sign photo) |

## Discrepancies to know about
- Head count: Grace's Guide quotes a period report of "about 70 officers and men"; a Taylor & Francis text says 60. Script uses 74 (Wikipedia / Manchester Guardian).
- Date: the Grace's Guide newspaper extract says "Monday the 11th"; most sources say 12 April. Script says only "1831", so this doesn't matter.
- The script says "16 feet" (lower of the 16-18 ft figure).

## Comic exaggeration (framing only, not presented as fact)
- "basically a trampoline with a river under it"
- "the best-timed push a bridge has ever felt"
- "the one thing an army can never do on a bridge... is march" (true rule is break step, i.e. no marching in step)
